How we scored this

We used the EU's General Data Protection Regulation as the benchmark: it's the most widely copied privacy law in the world. Each place gets 0 to 3 points on eleven criteria. Enforcement counts double, because a strong law nobody enforces protects no one. Government protections are scored separately so they don't hide how well a place protects you from companies.

    Where the facts come from: state law trackers from ClauseAudit, Morgan Lewis and MultiState for which laws exist and when they take effect; the Future of Privacy Forum's 2025 enforcement review and MultiState for enforcement actions; the ACLU and EPIC for police access to location data; and DLA Piper for breach notification laws.

    Limits: many scores are judgment calls, explained in each place's notes. Places with a broad law and no notable differences get the standard score for that kind of law. Police-access scores rely mainly on a 2015 survey and may miss newer laws. Enforcement reflects public actions through 2025, so states with new laws may simply not have acted yet.     Speak up

    More ways

    Other ways to get involved

    • Comment on proposed rules. When agencies write privacy rules they take public comments, and few people send them, so each one counts. Federal proposals are on Regulations.gov. California's privacy agency posts its rulemakings; other states' attorneys general post theirs on their own sites.
    • Follow privacy bills. Search for "privacy" or "data broker" on Congress.gov for federal bills and on Open States for your state's.
    • Speak at a hearing. State legislatures hold public hearings on bills, and many accept written testimony from residents. Your legislature's website lists hearing schedules.
    • Back a ballot initiative. In states that allow citizen initiatives, residents can put a law on the ballot directly. That's how California's privacy law began.
    • Support the groups doing this work, listed below.
